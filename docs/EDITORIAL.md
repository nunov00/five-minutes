# Five Minutes editorial standard

Status: accepted editorial standard. Owner calibration review was completed under issue #49 on 3 October 2026.

## Purpose

Five Minutes publishes one worthwhile idea at a time. The name is a promise of focus, not a stopwatch: there is no required reading time or word count. A lesson should be as short as it can be without weakening the explanation, and as long as it needs to be to earn the reader's time. A two-minute lesson and a ten-minute lesson can both meet the standard.

This rubric is the product contract for lesson generation and editorial review. Automated schema, safety and build checks remain separate gates. Passing them proves that a lesson is structurally publishable; it does not prove that the lesson is good.

## Publication standard

A publishable lesson must meet every criterion below. Reviewers decide readiness through judgement and written reasons against these criteria. The advisory ratings below never replace a criterion or average away a failure. A polished sentence cannot compensate for an unsupported claim, and a long source list cannot compensate for a dull or confusing lesson.

| Criterion | Publication standard | Common reasons to revise |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Factual accuracy and source support | Material claims are correct, qualified where needed and supported by sources suitable for the claim. Unstable facts are checked close to publication. Contested claims are presented as contested. | The source does not support the wording; a simplification changes the meaning; an attribution is repeated without checking the original; uncertainty is hidden. |
| Worth the reader's time | The lesson offers a surprising, useful or illuminating idea and makes clear why it matters. Every section advances that idea. | The topic is merely trivia; the opening promises more than the body delivers; the lesson lists facts without producing understanding. |
| Audience attention and sustained engagement | The title makes an honest, specific promise; the opening creates immediate curiosity or useful tension; and each paragraph moves the reader towards a satisfying payoff. Examples make the mechanism vivid, the explanation remains manageable for its intended readers, and novelty serves understanding rather than clickbait. | The lesson takes too long to reach its question; the opening is generic; momentum stalls; an example distracts from the mechanism; suspense hides essential context; the payoff does not fulfil the title's promise. |
| Clarity and accessibility | A reader with general education, including a reader using English as an additional language, can follow the explanation on one careful read. Sentences are direct, terms are defined on first use and cultural references are not required for comprehension. | Dense noun phrases, idioms, unexplained acronyms, long detours or examples that assume one country, school system or subculture. |
| Concision | The lesson contains no filler, throat-clearing, repeated conclusion or decorative detail that does not improve understanding. Length follows the idea rather than a target. | Multiple examples make the same point; the conclusion restates the whole article; qualifiers obscure a simple claim. |
| Explanation and examples | The lesson explains the mechanism or reasoning, not just the result. Examples are concrete, accurate and chosen to reveal the idea's limits as well as its basic form where useful. | An analogy replaces the explanation; an equation appears without interpretation; examples are technically false or too distant from the reader. |
| Structure and flow | The title sets an honest expectation. The opening establishes the question or tension, each paragraph follows from the last, and the ending resolves the opening. Headings are used only when they help navigation. | The key explanation arrives late; sections feel interchangeable; the lesson changes subject; a heading adds hierarchy without meaning. |
| Appropriate depth | The lesson includes what the reader needs to understand and use the idea, without assuming unexplained specialist knowledge. It names important limits but does not turn every caveat into a tangent. | A crucial causal step is skipped; jargon carries the argument; detail accumulates after the central idea is already complete. |
| Takeaway and retrieval question | The front-matter takeaway states the durable insight in one useful sentence and is rendered once by the reading page (after recall by default, after the opening lead for validated `answer-first` lessons); the body earns that conclusion without adding a second labelled takeaway. The question tests the insight; in an answer-first lesson it tests the further causal reasoning explained below. The answer explains that reasoning rather than rewarding a trivial word match. | The takeaway is repeated or contradicted in the body; it is a slogan; the question tests an isolated number or wording copied from the title; the answer introduces new unsupported information. |
| Memorability and payoff | A specific mental picture, example, connection or piece of reasoning makes the central insight easier to retain. The ending resolves the opening or reveals a worthwhile implication. | No clear answer to what the reader might remember tomorrow; a striking detail is unrelated to the mechanism; the ending is a recap list. |
| Explanatory depth | Writing is intelligent, economical and precise. Develop a useful causal step, technical concept, implication, trade-off or boundary beyond merely naming the idea. Explain terms immediately and assume no specialist knowledge. | Corporate or textbook tone; length or vocabulary substitutes for reasoning. |
| Title, deck and discoverability | Every published lesson has a specific, accurate title and a description that adds information. Search terms cover likely language a reader would use without stuffing synonyms. | Clickbait, vague titles, duplicated title/description, promises absent from the body or irrelevant search terms. |
| Originality and non-duplication | The lesson has a distinct central insight and treatment. Before publication, compare its identity, title, concepts, takeaway and examples with the catalogue. Overlap is acceptable only when the new lesson answers a materially different question. | A new title hides the same lesson; familiar material is paraphrased without a fresh explanatory angle; the treatment adds no fresh understanding. |

## Clear English for non-native readers

The published version must be understandable to a reader with intermediate English and general education, without repeatedly reaching for a dictionary. Follow British spelling and grammar. Prefer words widely understood internationally, and explain any necessary regional vocabulary. This applies to all reader-facing lesson text: titles, descriptions, prose, takeaway, retrieval questions and answers, activities and feedback, and figure labels, captions, alt text and full descriptions.

- Prefer familiar, concrete words and direct verbs: “use” rather than “utilise”, “makes it worse” rather than “exacerbates”. Choose the wording that preserves the exact meaning in context; simple words are not interchangeable in every sentence.
- Introduce a necessary technical term with a short explanation in ordinary language at first use in the lesson. Expand unfamiliar acronyms. Keep useful scientific vocabulary, but do not make a term carry reasoning that the text has not explained.
- Usually give each sentence one main idea. Split stacked clauses, dense noun phrases and long chains of qualifications. Vary sentence length naturally; no fixed word limit is required. Prefer active voice when it makes who does what clearer.
- Avoid unexplained idioms, slang, figurative phrasal verbs and cultural references needed to understand the point. For example, use “a rough estimate” rather than “a ballpark figure”. A familiar concrete analogy can still help when the lesson explains the actual relationship.
- Make connections explicit: show why one step causes the next, name what “this” or “it” refers to when ambiguous, and explain an unfamiliar comparison rather than expecting shared cultural knowledge.
- Preserve factual precision, uncertainty and essential qualifications. Gain depth through reasoning, implications and useful limits, not harder vocabulary. Use familiar examples and enough background without losing the complete explanation or becoming patronising.

For example, “Water’s thermal inertia mitigates diurnal temperature fluctuations” can become “Water heats up and cools down slowly. This reduces the temperature difference between day and night” when that is the relationship the lesson's sources support. This is an illustration of clearer wording, not approved factual copy for every context.

During the existing self-edit, read the lesson from the perspective of an intermediate English reader. Replace avoidable difficulty and check the surrounding explanation after rewriting. Reviewers independently ask: **Could a reader with intermediate English understand this without repeatedly reaching for a dictionary?** Record one language judgement for the lesson in the existing review, with a representative passage and its explained term or causal connection, or the concrete difficulty and proposed clearer wording. A generic “accessible” assertion is not evidence. Language is IMPORTANT when an intermediate English reader cannot follow a central explanation without repeatedly consulting a dictionary. Isolated wording that does not impair comprehension may be MINOR. A simplification that changes meaning is assessed under the existing factual-accuracy rules.

Readability tools may flag long or complex sentences, but are optional diagnostics only. Do not add a numeric pass mark, vocabulary quota, mandatory tool or extra review/task. A score cannot prove comprehension or justify removing accurate reasoning.

## Writing for understanding and memory

Build the lesson around one worthwhile insight, rather than a sequence of facts about a topic:

- **Give the reader a reason to care early.** A puzzle, surprising observation, recognisable situation or practical consequence can establish the question. Make the stakes concrete and proportionate; do not invent urgency or promise a life-changing application.
- **Explain the mechanism.** Connect cause and effect without skipping the step that makes the result intelligible. An analogy should help the reader follow that reasoning; the explanation must still work when the analogy is removed. If an analogy needs so many disclaimers that its image stops helping, replace it.
- **Choose something that sticks.** A mental picture, revealing example, useful comparison, sourced number or explanatory figure can anchor the insight. Choose what serves this topic. No lesson must contain every device, and no number or diagram should be added merely to meet a quota.
- **Show where the idea leads.** Develop a consequence, application or connection that helps the reader see something familiar differently. Keep it within the lesson's evidence and scope.
- **Deliver a payoff.** Resolve the opening question or draw out an implication. Avoid a final list that repeats the body; the reading page already provides the separate takeaway in the selected opening mode.

These are reader outcomes, not a compulsory five-part outline. Vary the structure and voice with the idea. All added facts, numbers and implications need the same source checking as the central explanation. Interesting wording never justifies overstating a claim.

## Figures and prediction activities

Every new lesson, migrated lesson and substantial editorial pass must explicitly assess whether each presentation would become clearer with a figure. Add one when a spatial, sequential, comparative, quantitative or causal relationship is materially easier to understand visually. Omit it when prose is already clearer, the image would be decorative, or no vetted app-owned visual style explains the idea faithfully; record that decision and its reason in the Pull Request. Figures remain conditional rather than a quota, but they must not be skipped by default. Never approximate an illustration with generic stacked text boxes merely to satisfy the assessment: no figure is better than a weak figure.

A figure is part of an individual presentation and is reviewed with the same factual standard as its prose. Reviewers verify every label, scale, relationship and caption against the lesson's sources, and confirm that required alt text communicates the explanatory purpose rather than merely naming the picture. Use the figure and caption that best explain the single lesson. Review the full-text description as an equivalent explanation, and mark schematic drawings as not to scale where appropriate. The concrete field shapes and limits are in [M6.2 reading enhancements](implementation/m6.2-reading-enhancements.md).

Figure review is performed on the exact-head rendered page at phone and wide viewports, not from front matter or component source alone. Before its description is expanded, the figure must communicate its basic relationship; labels must remain legible, unclipped and clear of meaningful artwork; numbered stages must visibly correspond to the mechanism; and the visual must earn the space it occupies. Automated containment, collision, clipping and overflow checks are necessary but cannot establish explanatory quality. Record `FIGURE: PASS`, `FIGURE: OMIT` or `FIGURE: REWORK` for the lesson with the inspected route, viewports and reason. An included figure that needs its prose description merely to decode the drawing is `REWORK`, normally an IMPORTANT finding.

ADR-0014 adds up to five positioned visuals with a single optional raster lead, retaining the singular field for existing lessons. Use the figure toolkit's collection and provenance contract; this changes capability, not the requirement to justify each visual or complete exact-head visual review. Generated illustrations must be described truthfully and never serve as factual evidence. Five is a ceiling, not a target or a minimum: use fewer when that is clearer, and require every visual to explain a distinct relationship, remain readable on a phone and sit beside the relevant text. Avoid combining unrelated explanations into an oversized image merely to reduce the count. Source rasters share the existing 1500 KiB total budget; generation requests retain their separate ceilings.

Only the safe representations defined by ADR-0006 and the reusable [figure toolkit](implementation/figure-toolkit.md) are publishable: application-rendered diagrams from validated structured data or local PNG/WebP assets within configured size/file-count limits. Structured figures must select a named, vetted visual style/pattern and meaningful semantic symbols matching the lesson; the Writer cannot supply colours, coordinates or drawing instructions. Lesson-authored SVG, HTML, remote images and executable embeds are not accepted. A useful diagram blocked by missing capability or verification must be recorded as `VISUAL GAP`, with a concrete proposal and missing evidence/capability; assess the static fallback before omitting it. For rasters in all daily candidates (including revised imported lessons) and in other non-imported lessons, persist creation/origin/licence evidence in per-asset imageRights records in lesson.yaml; verify those records against actual evidence rather than accepting self-attestation. Strip text/EXIF/XMP/private metadata and ICC profiles. For each raster, verify browser decoding and natural dimensions, and never treat container validation as proof of visual correctness.

Every new or migrated lesson and substantial editorial pass must explicitly assess whether a lightweight prediction or choice would help the reader engage with the mechanism. Include an activity when committing to an answer, ordering possibilities or choosing between plausible explanations makes the subsequent explanation more effective. Omit it, with the reason recorded in the Pull Request, when it would merely repeat **Test yourself**, interrupt the flow or force artificial gamification. Activities are invitations to think, not a quota or decoration.

Reviewers verify that the prompt is unambiguous, every option is plausible in context, the marked/recommended answer is correct, and the explanation follows from the lesson without introducing unsupported claims. Wrong options should express plausible misconceptions that the explanation helps resolve, rather than be obviously absurd or give away the answer through wording. Apply that principle to any choice-based retrieval question too; open questions need no invented options. The activity must remain understandable and revealable without JavaScript and must not patronise the reader.

## Research and source practice

Prefer primary, official or otherwise authoritative sources for factual foundations. A secondary source may help explain or contextualise a subject, but it should not be the only support for a claim it merely repeats. Match each source to the claims it can actually establish.

Check dates for facts that can change. For contested or consequential claims, seek independent support and describe the disagreement or uncertainty. Do not turn a popular paraphrase into a verbatim quotation or imply that a later formulation appeared in an original work.

### Reader-facing source references

For new lessons and substantial editorial revisions, connect the central explanation, material experimental/historical claims, important numbers and factual diagrams to their supporting sources. Use a small number of references beside the relevant sentence or paragraph; do not cite every sentence or replace readable explanation with citations. Prefer original research for reported experiments, and authoritative reviews, textbooks or official accounts for established explanations and context. A primary source is not automatically the most suitable source for every claim.

Keep the bibliography in the existing `lesson.yaml` `sources` array. The application displays it as a numbered **Sources** section after recall. In body Markdown, write `[^1]` to link to the first entry, `[^2]` to the second, and so on. The same source can be cited repeatedly. There are no Markdown footnote definitions or separate body bibliography. Keep tokens out of headings, link labels and all presentation fields except figure captions/descriptions; use body prose to support a takeaway rather than placing a token inside its plain-text field. Code remains literal. Recheck all references if the array order changes. Ordinary descriptive external links remain available where useful. Return from a source to the reading position with the browser's Back action; no generated return link guesses which of several citations the reader came from.

Record the known author or organisation and publication year in the source `title` alongside the work's title; retain `publisher`, direct HTTP(S) `url` and the actual `accessedAt` date. Do not mistake access date for publication date or invent missing dates/authorship. Use the optional plain-text `note` for a concise reader-facing explanation of what the source supports, with the relevant page, figure or section when useful. Disclose abstract-only or other material access limits there. Cite only material actually checked, and never imply full-paper verification from a search result or abstract. Research audit details can stay on the PR. Existing notes retain their original research wording as visibly secondary source detail, including access limits; they are not claimed to have received a new factual/editorial audit. Improve that wording and check bibliographic publisher attribution during each lesson's next reviewed substantive revision, preserving the claim support and access record.

Factual explanatory diagrams need a source reference in their caption, using the same `[^N]` token; descriptions may also use it. Keep schematic/not-to-scale and conceptual-artwork labels truthful. A citation supports the explanation, not a claim that an original drawing is a photograph, copied source figure or measured simulation. Source references do not replace image-rights records. Do not add citation tokens to alt text or diagram labels.

Reviewers check each reference against the exact wording or figure, the source content and the stated access limits. Inspect the rendered numbered links and Sources entries on phone and wide layouts, including keyboard/no-JavaScript navigation. Broken targets fail validation; misleading support is a factual finding under the existing severity rules. Missing editorially necessary references require revision. Existing unmodified lessons show their recorded source lists without automatic claim-to-source assignments; backfill references during reviewed substantive revision rather than fabricating them in bulk. No new publication authority, task or review round is created.

## One complete lesson

Publish one version for curious readers without specialist knowledge. Use familiar examples, clear English and a complete causal explanation. Develop one useful additional implication, limit or trade-off when it helps understanding. Depth comes from reasoning rather than difficult vocabulary or extra length. There is no comparison against another audience version.

## Review outcomes

Review findings use the project-wide severity language:

- **BLOCKER** — publication would be unsafe or untrustworthy. Examples include a material factual error, invented or misrepresented source, harmful instruction, plagiarism, identity/route conflict or a missing lesson source. The lesson must not publish.
- **IMPORTANT** — the lesson does not yet meet the editorial contract. Examples include an unsupported central claim, confusing causal gap, misleading title, weak retrieval question, patronising voice or substantial duplication. An accurate but generic lesson also needs revision when it lacks a clear reason to care, a memorable explanation or a satisfying payoff, or when the explanation adds no meaningful understanding beyond naming the idea. Identify the missing reader outcome and a targeted revision; do not dismiss the gap as stylistic preference. Revise before publication.
- **MINOR** — local polish that does not undermine accuracy, comprehension or the lesson's value. It may publish if the finding is explicitly recorded and accepted under the project's merge rules.

For a narrow correction to a published lesson that is not a substantial editorial pass, pre-existing weaknesses in interest, memorability/payoff or explanatory depth may be deferred only when the correction neither introduces nor worsens them. Record those weaknesses in a linked follow-up issue rather than requiring an unrelated rewrite before the correction can merge. Review the lesson and retain all other applicable gates, including factual accuracy, source support, safety, essential causal reasoning and figure verification. New lessons and substantial editorial passes must meet the full reader-value standard.

A lesson is **Ready** only when there are no unresolved BLOCKER or IMPORTANT findings and all automated gates pass. When evidence is uncertain, the reviewer fails closed and requests revision or escalation rather than converting uncertainty into a low rating.

Accuracy, usefulness and attention are separate gates. They are not averaged: an entertaining factual error, a useful lesson that loses its audience before the explanation, or an absorbing lesson with no durable value is not Ready. Attention must be maximised within the constraints of truth, accessibility and respect for the reader; manipulative suspense, sensational claims and engagement tricks do not qualify.

## Required review record

For factual and editorial review, record:

1. the exact lesson commit reviewed and the reviewer identity/system;
2. the outcome: Ready, Revise or Block;
3. findings classified as BLOCKER, IMPORTANT or MINOR;
4. for each finding, the relevant criterion, concrete passage or claim, rationale and required action;
5. source checks performed and material uncertainty that remains;
6. a judgement on the complete single lesson;
7. attention evidence for the lesson: the title promise, opening hook or tension, points where momentum or comprehension may drop, and whether the payoff fulfils the promise;
8. the catalogue comparison used for originality and duplication.
9. the figure decision for the lesson and the prediction activity decision, including the reason for any omission; for an included figure, record `FIGURE: PASS` or `FIGURE: REWORK`, the exact-head rendered route and the phone/wide viewports inspected.
10. a concise reader-value assessment, with passage-specific evidence rather than a generic assertion, answering these four questions:
    - **Interest:** would a curious non-expert with no prior interest choose to open this lesson from its title and description, and keep reading? Explain the concrete reason to care, the reward for reading and any passage where interest drops. This is an editorial hypothesis unless supported by actual reader evidence.
    - **Understanding:** how does the lesson explain the mechanism, and is a crucial causal step missing?
    - **Memory:** what specific image, example, connection or reasoning might each reader remember tomorrow, and does the ending deliver its payoff? This is editorial judgement, not a claim of measured retention.
    - **Depth:** what useful reasoning, implication or boundary does the explanation develop?

11. one language-accessibility judgement for the lesson under **Clear English for non-native readers**, with passage-specific evidence and actionable rewrites where needed.

These answers can be incorporated into the existing lesson assessment and attention notes; do not duplicate the review. Missing reader outcomes require actionable findings under the severity definitions above. Check the author's claims against the actual text rather than accepting their self-assessment.

Include two brief **advisory ratings**, each a whole number from 1 to 10, within the existing reader-value assessment for lesson reviews, including scheduled Reviewer assessments, same-provider separate-pass reviews and re-reviews. Reuse the Interest and other reader-value evidence rather than writing it twice. On re-review, reassess lesson quality against the current head; topic appeal may be carried forward from a cited earlier assessment if the question and angle are unchanged. State which ratings were reassessed or carried forward. This does not expand a narrow correction into an unrelated editorial rewrite; the correction exception above still applies.

- **Topic appeal:** how compelling is the central question and angle for a curious non-expert with no prior interest, assuming it is explained well? Identify the reason to choose it and the potential reward for reading.
- **Lesson quality:** how well does the actual finished lesson deliver that promise through its explanation, language, structure, figures where useful and ending? Support the judgement with concrete passages and reader outcomes.

For each, explain what holds it back and ask: **“What would raise this by roughly two points, or towards 10 if already near the top?”** For the ratings, highlight at most two highest-value improvements and identify whether each concerns the topic/angle, its execution or both. This limit covers only the ratings' improvement suggestions. The findings record must still include every BLOCKER, IMPORTANT and MINOR finding, including any beyond those two suggestions. The proposed changes and reader benefit matter more than the numbers; do not promise a measured two-point gain. Distinguish required fixes under the existing severity rules from optional polish. If fewer than two worthwhile improvements remain, say so rather than inventing work. If either aspect cannot be assessed, record that limitation instead of inventing a rating.

For every new lesson PR and each material lesson revision, the author must record an **Author quality assessment** as a separate top-level PR comment headed `Author quality assessment @ <full candidate SHA>` before handing that head to the Reviewer. This comment is the single location for the self-assessment; do not put its numerical ratings in the PR body, review handoff or other introductory notes. Handoffs may link to the comment by heading/SHA without quoting the numbers. This supersedes the former prohibition on author self-ratings. Name the exact candidate SHA, author/provider and assessment time; give separate whole-number topic-appeal and lesson-quality ratings with passage-specific reasons, actual phone/wide rendered-figure evidence and any source/render limitations. State what still prevents lesson quality reaching the Owner's 9+/10 ambition, or justify why it meets it; identify at most two highest-value improvements and check whether this revision introduced a regression. Reassess lesson quality on each changed material head; carry topic appeal forward only with a cited prior assessment and a reason the angle is unchanged. If evidence is missing, record the assessment as incomplete rather than inventing a score. Do not apply lesson ratings to code-only, UI-only or documentation-only PRs, or expand a narrow correction beyond its existing review scope.

Form and record the initial self-assessment before consulting external scores where possible. Never skip required history/feedback checks to claim blindness; disclose any earlier-score exposure. Once external review arrives, retain the original self-assessment and append a short comparison explaining material disagreements, missed defects and the resulting actions. A self-assessment is author evidence, never a qualifying independent review or permission to publish.

The Manager must verify that a complete assessment names the current material lesson head before reporting author work complete or handing it off as fully assessed. The Reviewer records a missing, stale or explicitly incomplete assessment as a **MINOR process finding** requiring the author to supply current-head evidence. This finding alone does not change the lesson's editorial Ready/Revise/Block outcome; the Manager must still resolve the missing author evidence before reporting author work complete. Any underlying factual, accessibility or editorial defect receives its own normal severity and outcome. Supplying assessment evidence alone does not authorise or consume an extra content-revision round.

**Reviewer order:** record initial topic and lesson ratings and reasons from the lesson, sources and rendered page before deliberately opening the author's self-assessment body. First check its heading/SHA using an index or filtered tool output that omits the body; a link in the handoff must not quote author scores. Then read and challenge the self-assessment and append any resulting change in judgement with reasons, preserving the initial ratings. This applies to independent, same-provider separate-pass and Coordinator child reviews. Required feedback/history checks still happen in their prescribed order: do not skip or postpone them to claim blindness. If necessary history, bulk retrieval or an inherited context already exposed author/earlier scores, disclose that exposure and do not claim a blind assessment. Score separation reduces anchoring; it cannot guarantee independence of numerical judgement.

Technical test results do not establish editorial quality. The 9+ ambition is not a new numerical publication threshold, an excuse to inflate scores or authority for extra revisions, generations, tasks or budget. Existing factual/accessibility Ready gates, independent-review requirements and production limits remain unchanged. This is a procedural evidence requirement; CI does not validate subjective scores.

**Transition:** this author-evidence requirement applies to new/materially revised lesson heads handed off after the amendment merges. Heads already in flight are not retrospectively marked deficient for an earlier stage. At the next otherwise authorised assessment of an existing open head, supply a current-head self-assessment labelled `transition assessment`, disclosing all earlier-score exposure; do not invent a historical or blind assessment. This evidence-only action adds no production opportunity and does not reset or consume content-revision budgets. For the controlled weekly trial, activation and transition wait for the merged contract and verified saved-prompt readback on #200 as specified there.

If the Owner and reviewers agree a published reference lesson and rating, cite its exact version and the reasons for that benchmark when using it. Until then, do not invent a shared reference or assume that the same number means the same thing across reviewers. This adds no separate calibration task or panel.

The ratings are subjective diagnostics, not measured reader satisfaction, publication thresholds or approvals. Do not combine or average them, add decimal precision or use either number to override accuracy, accessibility, Ready/Revise/Block or any existing gate. A low rating alone is not an actionable finding; a high rating cannot excuse one. Existing revision limits still apply: do not create an extra review or revision loop to chase a higher number. Do not report precision-style confidence numbers. Descriptive confidence is useful only when it explains what is uncertain and what evidence would resolve it.

The canonical review record is the GitHub pull-request review on the exact commit. Any accepted MINOR finding and its rationale must be recorded in the same PR conversation or merge record. `provenance.reviews` may preserve earlier lesson-level review or substantive-revision events already incorporated into the reviewed commit; it is not updated after the final exact-head review, because doing so would create a new unreviewed head.

## Challenge the topic before drafting

This section governs Owner-approved pilots and, after activation, weekly topic selection. The existing daily workflow continues to follow [the ranked pipeline](editorial-pipeline.md); it gains no pre-draft rating requirement from this section.

Before investing in lesson prose or artwork, compare the leading three eligible candidates using their proposed question/angle, honest headline, short description, reader payoff and visual concept. Assess the treatment rather than a broad subject label. Check evidence, scope, duplication and production feasibility first under the applicable pipeline; appeal cannot rescue an ineligible candidate.

In the existing selection brief or planning discussion, the selection reviewer gives one advisory **topic-appeal rating (1–10)** per candidate and answers:

- Why would a curious non-expert choose this over competing content?
- What familiar experience, useful implication, compelling story or worthwhile understanding makes it matter? Immediate practical use is not required.
- What is the surprising explanation or payoff, and can the opening image communicate the subject and reason to care honestly?
- What holds the angle back, and what would raise it by roughly two points, or towards 10? Name at most two worthwhile changes; do not invent work.
- Would improving the angle be enough, or would a competing candidate make a stronger lesson?

Give short reasons and a comparative recommendation, not a numerical ranking formula. Record unresolved evidence or feasibility limits and develop/defer an unsuitable angle before drafting. Do not rate an unwritten lesson's quality. Apply the existing advisory-rating rules above: independent initial judgement where possible, prior-score exposure disclosed, author self-assessment on finished lesson heads as required above, agreed exact-version benchmarks only, no thresholds/averages or new revision loops. AI assessments are editorial hypotheses, not measured demand.

Reuse this selection evidence in the later lesson review, reassessing topic appeal if the angle changes; assess actual lesson quality separately on the finished exact head. Keep this within existing selection/review work: no extra scheduled task, reader panel, per-topic PR, schema field or publication authority. Weekly shortlist changes remain subject to the weekly process's separate implementation boundary.

## Drafting and critical self-edit

Before drafting, prepare a short working brief: the worthwhile question, the reasoning that answers it, a concrete reader connection or memory anchor, and the supported additional understanding the explanation will develop. A named fact, longer wording or technical label is not an additional explanation. If the causal chain or deeper reasoning cannot be explained and sourced, improve the angle before writing. This brief guides the work; it is not a fixed lesson outline or reader-facing text.

After drafting, read the single version as a finished lesson. Ask whether a curious non-expert would choose to open it and keep reading, and identify the reward the text actually delivers; record the brief evidence in the existing reader-value assessment. Identify the actual passages delivering the hook, causal steps, payoff and explanatory depth. Test whether the reasoning still works without the analogy. Check retrieval against the central takeaway and challenge every activity distractor as a plausible misconception. Remove self-assessments such as "the memorable reversal" and let the explanation earn its value. Revise missing reader outcomes before requesting review; filling out a quality checklist is not evidence that the text achieves them. Use the calibrated examples below to judge substance while varying topics and voice.

### Challenge the first draft before submission

Apply these three checks to the actual draft during the existing critical self-edit. Fix failures before opening the PR. Record the brief evidence in the PR's existing reader-value/activity assessment; do not add another review, task or separate scorecard/checklist document; put the required Author quality assessment only in its separate `Author quality assessment @ <full candidate SHA>` comment. Keep numerical self-ratings out of the PR body and handoff. This is author evidence for the separate Reviewer to challenge, never publication approval.

1. **Explanatory depth:** finish “After this lesson, the reader can explain ___ and why it happens.” Point to the passage that supplies the reasoning and explain the added understanding in plain language without relying on its technical names. Then mentally remove that passage: if the reader loses only vocabulary, arithmetic detail or length, develop a worthwhile causal step, implication, trade-off or boundary. A worked example counts when it reveals additional reasoning, not merely because it contains numbers. Preserve the complete central explanation.
2. **Concrete meaning and coherence:** challenge claims such as “more efficient”, “a quoted percentage”, “stronger protection” or “a significant cost”. Can the reader explain what changes, relative to what, and why it matters from this text? Use a supported comparison, worked example, familiar consequence or useful numerical context where it answers that question. Check units, denominators, conditions and limits when using figures. Resolve references whose antecedent or example is missing. Numbers are optional; decorative precision and unsupported universal rules are failures too.
3. **Believable wrong answers:** for each distractor, name the specific misconception a reader could reasonably hold before the explanation. Read the prompt and options without the key or explanatory feedback: could the answer be selected just by dismissing a silly option, spotting a wording clue or choosing the only relevant statement? Replace weak options and check that the explanation resolves the misconceptions. Simplify or omit an optional activity when plausible alternatives would be artificial; do not pad it to a fixed option count. A wrong option may be factually false, but must be believable at the point where the reader sees it.

Give passage-specific evidence: the explanatory passage and added reasoning; the concrete anchor or resolved ambiguity for the main explanation; and the misconception behind each included wrong option. Incorporate this into the existing PR notes rather than repeating the whole lesson. Do not claim that passing these checks guarantees reader understanding.

Calibration from the QR lesson review, PR #165 (19 October 2026): “polynomials over a finite set of values” did not explain Adult's extra layer; explaining the unknown error's position and change amount supplied it. Referring to a correction percentage before giving one left a missing reference. “The phone can ignore the square pattern” was an implausible capacity distractor; “the same-sized code can hold a longer message” expressed a misconception the explanation could resolve. These diagnose drafting weaknesses; they are not universal prescriptions about coding theory or requirements to add mathematics to other topics.

### Revising after review

Apply the three first-draft checks and the clear-English self-edit above to any passage or option the revision adds or changes.

For a requested rewrite, assess all applicable findings together, re-fetch sources for changed material claims and explain each finding's disposition with passage/evidence references. Disagree with unsupported suggestions explicitly rather than adding jargon, numbers or tangents automatically. Preserve the complete central explanation when adding depth. The later Reviewer verifies the revised result independently. ADR-0007 and the task contract bound scheduled unpublished-candidate revisions; this editorial guidance never grants merge authority.

## Author checklist

Before requesting review:

- state the central insight in one sentence;
- state the title's promise and ensure the opening reaches the interesting question immediately;
- identify the concrete reason to care and the image, example, connection or reasoning the reader could remember tomorrow;
- verify the important claims against the recorded sources;
- remove any paragraph that does not improve understanding;
- define necessary terms and test the explanation without its analogy;
- check that each paragraph creates progress and that the ending delivers the promised payoff;
- read the lesson independently for flow, voice and intermediate-English accessibility under **Clear English for non-native readers**, including its metadata, questions, activities and figure text;
- identify the explanatory depth and check that the central causal chain is complete;
- verify that the takeaway and retrieval answer express the same durable insight;
- compare the proposed lesson with the existing catalogue for overlap;
- assess a useful figure for the lesson and a prediction activity; include supported additions when they improve understanding, otherwise record why they were omitted;
- inspect every included figure on the rendered page at phone and wide widths, checking meaning before expansion, collisions, clipping, hierarchy and connection between labels/stages and artwork;
- run the canonical content, build and static validation gates.
