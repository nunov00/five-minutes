Your phone can find you because several clocks in space disagree with clocks on Earth in a predictable way.

GPS satellites send signals containing their position and the exact time of transmission. Your receiver compares when several signals arrive. Since radio signals move at the speed of light, their travel times reveal the receiver's distance from each satellite. Even a difference of a few billionths of a second can shift the calculated distance by metres.

Here is the strange part: two effects change the satellite clocks in opposite directions. Their rapid motion makes them run about 7 microseconds per day slower relative to Earth. But gravity is weaker at their altitude, which makes them run about 45 microseconds per day faster.

The second effect is larger, leaving a net gain of about 38 microseconds per day. GPS accounts for this from the start. If it did not, the growing timing mismatch would quickly make the system useless for navigation.
