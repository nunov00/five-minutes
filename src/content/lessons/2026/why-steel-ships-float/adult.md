A cargo ship can rise a little as it leaves a fresh-water river for the sea, even with the same load aboard. Seawater is denser: the same volume weighs more. The ship can therefore support its weight by pushing aside less water. But how does water support a steel ship in the first place?[^3][^6]

## Where the upward push comes from

Water presses on every surface it touches. Its pressure increases with depth because the water above has weight. A submerged block therefore feels a stronger upward water push on its bottom than the downward push on its top.[^1]

The sideways pushes balance, but the vertical pushes leave an overall upward force. This is **buoyancy**. It acts on a sinking bolt too; the bolt sinks because that upward force is smaller than its weight.[^2]

## Weigh the missing water

Imagine removing an underwater object and filling its place with water. That replacement water would stay supported by the surrounding water. The surrounding water gives the object the same overall upward push: equal to the weight of the water that would fill that space. This is **Archimedes’ principle**.[^2]

The water pushed aside is called **displaced water**. A bolt occupies little space for its weight. Even completely submerged, it cannot displace water weighing as much as itself. Going deeper raises pressure on both top and bottom; in water of nearly constant density, it does not keep increasing the difference between those pushes.[^1][^2]

A hull changes the comparison. Its thin walls keep water out of a much larger space, including the air-filled interior. Only the part below the water surface counts as displaced volume. A floating ship at rest settles where that displaced water weighs as much as the ship, cargo and everything else aboard. The steel itself has not become less dense.[^3]

## What changes when cargo arrives?

Before reading on, predict what happens when more cargo is added to the same floating hull: does the water need to push harder, or is its upward force fixed by the hull’s shape?

The water must push harder. The load increases the ship’s weight, so it initially moves down. More hull then lies below the water surface, displacing more water. If there is enough room before water enters, the ship can settle at a new, lower position where the forces balance.[^3][^4]

For a simple example, take a box-shaped hull and its contents with a total mass of 2,000 kg. In fresh water, roughly 1,000 kg of water occupies 1 cubic metre. The hull must therefore displace about 2 cubic metres to float at rest. Add 1,000 kg of cargo and it needs about 3 cubic metres: one extra cubic metre of water pushed aside. These are calculated example values, not measurements of a real ship.[^1][^3]

That adjustment has limits. As the edge gets closer to the water, less margin remains before water can spill inside. Staying upright is a separate problem too: uneven loading can tip a boat even when it has enough space to displace the required water.[^4]

## The marks painted on a ship

Look near the middle of a large cargo ship’s side and you may see a circle crossed by a horizontal line, beside several short lines and letters. These are load-line marks. They show loading limits that help preserve a margin of hull above the water. The limits also depend on the season and where the ship travels.[^5][^6]

The letters connect directly to our puzzle. S marks the summer salt-water line; F marks the summer fresh-water line. Fresh water is less dense than seawater, so the same volume weighs less. The ship still needs to push aside water equal to its own weight. In fresh water that requires a larger volume, so more of the hull goes underwater. The F mark is higher on the hull because the water surface reaches further up its side.[^1][^3][^6]

A ship can therefore rise as it leaves a fresh-water river for the sea without unloading any cargo. Next time you spot those small painted lines, they are more than a warning about weight: they also record that the water doing the supporting can change.[^3][^6]
