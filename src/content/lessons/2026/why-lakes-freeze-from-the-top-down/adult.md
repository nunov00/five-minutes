On a cold day, a shallow puddle may freeze solid. A deep lake usually behaves differently: ice spreads across its surface while liquid water remains underneath. The reason combines circulation, an unusual property of water and the insulating effect of ice.

In early winter, the air cools the lake from above. Cooler surface water becomes denser and sinks, displacing slightly warmer water that rises and is cooled in turn. This circulation continues as the surface approaches the freezing point. Because liquid water is densest at 0°C, the coldest water settles beneath the forming ice.

## A solid that floats

When water freezes, its molecules form an open crystal lattice. The solid occupies more volume for the same mass, so ice is about nine per cent less dense than liquid water. The first ice therefore stays at the surface rather than sinking.

That floating layer changes what happens next. Ice conducts heat much less effectively than freely mixing liquid water, and snow on top can add still more insulation. Heat can still escape, so the ice thickens downward during sustained cold, but progressively more slowly.

A lake is not guaranteed to stay safely liquid. Shallow water can freeze through, wind can disturb early ice, and dissolved salts alter density and freezing behaviour. Depth, weather, inflowing water and snow cover all matter.

Still, the basic sequence is powerful: cooling drives circulation, freezing creates buoyant ice, and the ice then protects some of the water below. This lets many aquatic organisms survive winter in liquid habitat beneath a frozen surface.
