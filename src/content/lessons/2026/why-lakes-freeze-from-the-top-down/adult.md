Ice floating explains why a lake's frozen lid stays on top. It leaves another question: before any ice exists, why does the coldest liquid water stay there long enough to freeze?

In autumn, cooling surface water usually becomes denser and sinks. Warmer water rises to replace it. That circulation helps carry heat from deeper water towards the air. If cooling always made fresh water denser, this process would keep sending the coldest surface water downwards.

But fresh water reaches its greatest density at about **4°C**, several degrees above freezing. Below that point, further cooling makes it less dense. Surface water approaching 0°C can therefore sit above denser water closer to 4°C. The coldest water no longer has an automatic route to the bottom.

## The change from mixing to layering

This creates **stable stratification**: layers arranged with less-dense water above denser water. To exchange their positions, a disturbance must push lighter water down and lift heavier water up against buoyancy. That requires energy. The layers are a resistance to mixing, rather than an invisible solid wall.

Wind can still stir the open surface. Once ice forms, however, the lid removes that direct wind stirring. A typical ice-covered temperate lake can retain colder liquid near the top and warmer, denser water deeper down. Not every lake settles to exactly 4°C throughout its depths; its shape, dissolved substances and water movement matter.

The ice floats and also adds an insulating barrier. It does not seal heat in permanently, but liquid water can persist below while the surface remains frozen.

## A refuge with a limit

For fish, liquid habitat is only part of the winter problem. Ice cover restricts oxygen exchange with the air. Organisms keep using oxygen, including microbes decomposing material on the bottom, so some waters become dangerously oxygen-poor.

That gives seasonal mixing another job. When the density barrier weakens and wind can mix the water, oxygen from near the surface can reach deeper layers. The familiar spring-and-autumn pattern occurs in many temperate lakes, rather than in every lake worldwide.

The frozen lid hides a change that happened before freezing: cooling first helped mix the lake, then helped separate it. That reversal leaves room for winter life, though it cannot guarantee survival.
