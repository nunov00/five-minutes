A lake in winter can have people skating on top and fish swimming below. That seems strange: if the air is cold enough to make ice, why does the whole lake not freeze at once?

The cooling starts at the surface. As the top water gets colder, it becomes denser and sinks. Warmer water rises to replace it, cools, and sinks too. This circulation keeps moving heat towards the air. Liquid water reaches its greatest density at 0°C, so the coldest water gathers below the surface as freezing begins.

Then water does something unusual. When it freezes, its molecules lock into a crystal pattern that leaves extra space between them. Ice is therefore less dense than liquid water, so it floats.

## The lake gets a lid

Once a sheet of ice forms, it separates the water from the cold air. Ice transfers heat more slowly than churning liquid water, and snow can make the lid even more insulating. More ice can grow underneath, but the process slows as the layer thickens.

This is not magic protection. A shallow pond may freeze through, moving water can weaken ice, and thickness can vary dramatically over a few metres. Never assume a frozen surface is safe just because it looks solid.

For the lake ecosystem, though, floating ice matters. It leaves liquid water underneath where fish and other organisms can survive until spring.
