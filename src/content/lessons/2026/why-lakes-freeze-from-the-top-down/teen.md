A lake can have ice on top and fish swimming underneath. But here is the puzzle before the ice: if cold water sinks, why does the coldest water not end up at the bottom?

For much of autumn, it does sink. The air cools the surface, the cooling water becomes denser, and it moves down. Warmer water rises to replace it. This movement helps the lake lose heat.

Then the rule changes. Fresh water is densest at about **4°C**. Cool it further and it becomes less dense, even though it is getting colder.

So water near 0°C can stay above water nearer 4°C. The lake now has a cold surface layer that can reach freezing without continually sinking away.

## A lid above a liquid room

Ice is less dense than liquid water, so it floats when it forms. The lid also keeps wind from directly stirring the water below and slows its loss of heat. That helps liquid water remain underneath.

The picture is not identical in every lake. Depth, weather and water movement affect the layers. Nor does a frozen surface tell you it is safe to walk on: appearance alone cannot establish ice strength.

Fish can live in the liquid water below, but they still need oxygen. With ice restricting exchange with the air, that supply can run low while fish and other organisms use it.

The useful surprise happens before water turns solid. “Colder sinks” works only up to a point. Near 4°C the pattern reverses, helping leave the lake with a frozen roof and a liquid room underneath.
