---
title: Why Shadows Change Through the Day
description: A shadow records the changing direction and height of the Sun across the sky.
takeaway: Shadow direction and length change because the apparent position of the Sun changes as Earth rotates.
question: Why is a vertical object's shadow usually shortest near local solar noon?
answer: The Sun is then near its highest point in the sky, so its light reaches the object at a steeper angle and casts a shorter shadow.
searchTerms: [changing shadows, sun position, solar noon]
---
Place a vertical stick in sunlight and mark the end of its shadow every hour. The marks will not stay together: they trace a curve across the ground.

A shadow points away from the Sun because the object blocks light travelling from the Sun. As Earth rotates, the Sun appears to move across the sky, so the incoming light changes direction and the shadow turns.

Length changes too. When the Sun is low, light arrives at a shallow angle and the shadow stretches. Near local solar noon, the Sun is usually at its highest point for that day, so the same fixed object casts a shorter shadow.

The exact pattern depends on latitude, season and the date. That is why a sundial needs its geometry matched to its location rather than using one universal set of hour marks.
