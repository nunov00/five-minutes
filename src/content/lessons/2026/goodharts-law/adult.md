A call centre wants customers to get help quickly, so it rewards staff for keeping calls short. Average call time falls. But some problems now require repeat calls, and customers spend longer getting an answer.

The measure improved while the result got worse.

This pattern is often summarised as Goodhart's law: when a measure becomes a target, it tends to stop being a good measure. The wording is a later shorthand for an observation Charles Goodhart made about monetary policy. Once officials tried to control particular monetary measures, the relationships that had made those measures useful became unstable.

## The gap between a goal and its proxy

Most important goals are hard to measure directly. “Customers solved their problem” takes follow-up and judgement. Call duration is immediate and countable, so it becomes a **proxy**: a measurable stand-in for the result that matters.

The proxy works while it moves with the real goal. It becomes unreliable when people are rewarded for the number itself. They may game the rule, neglect unmeasured parts of the job or discover a shortcut that raises the score without producing the desired result.

That does not make metrics useless. It means a metric needs context. Pair speed with measures of repeat calls and customer outcomes. Audit surprising improvements. Ask what behaviour the target rewards, and revise it when that behaviour changes.
