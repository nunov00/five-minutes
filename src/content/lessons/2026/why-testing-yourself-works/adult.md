---
title: Why Testing Yourself Beats Another Reread
description: Retrieving an idea from memory strengthens later recall and reveals gaps that familiar-looking notes can hide.
takeaway: To make knowledge easier to retrieve later, practise retrieving it now.
question: Why can a closed-book self-test improve learning rather than merely measure it?
answer: The effort of reconstructing knowledge strengthens later access to it and exposes what cannot yet be recalled.
searchTerms: [retrieval practice, testing effect, how to study]
---
Rereading feels productive because the page becomes familiar. But familiarity answers the wrong question: “Do I recognise this?” An exam, conversation or real decision asks, “Can I produce it when the page is gone?”

**Retrieval practice** means attempting that production deliberately. Close the book and explain the idea, answer a question or write what you remember. Then check the result and correct it.

In a well-known study, students either restudied prose passages or repeatedly recalled them. Restudy helped on a test five minutes later, but retrieval produced better retention after two days and one week. The test was not only a measurement; retrieving changed later memory.

Retrieval also supplies honest feedback. A smooth reread can hide a missing causal step, while a blank answer makes the gap visible. Feedback matters: check mistakes before practising them again.

This does not mean replacing understanding with endless quizzes. First build a clear model, then practise reconstructing and applying it at increasing intervals.

**Takeaway:** memory improves when study resembles the future act of remembering.
