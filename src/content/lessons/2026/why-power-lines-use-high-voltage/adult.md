A power line loses energy because its metal has resistance. Moving current through that resistance heats the wire. The heating loss is proportional to the square of the current: double the current and the loss becomes four times as large.

The useful relationship is that electrical power equals voltage multiplied by current. To transmit one megawatt at 10 kilovolts requires 100 amperes. At 100 kilovolts, the same power requires 10 amperes. In the same resistance, one-tenth of the current produces one-hundredth of the heating loss.

## Raise it, move it, lower it

Power systems therefore raise voltage before long-distance transmission. For alternating current, transformers make this practical: a step-up transformer raises the voltage near the generator, and other transformers lower it in stages before electricity reaches homes and businesses.

Higher voltage is not free. It needs more insulation, greater clearance and equipment designed to prevent dangerous electrical arcs. Engineers choose a voltage by balancing those costs against the savings from lower current and lower loss.

**Takeaway:** transmission lines use high voltage so they can carry useful power without wasting as much of it as heat.
