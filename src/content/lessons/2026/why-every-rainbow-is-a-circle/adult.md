A rainbow appears to touch a field. Walk towards that spot and the coloured end refuses to become an object you can reach. The field has an address; the rainbow does not.

Sunlight entering a water droplet bends, reflects inside it and bends again on leaving. This bending is **refraction**. Different wavelengths bend by different amounts, separating the light into colours.

For a primary rainbow, the light reaching your eye is especially concentrated near particular angles: red appears about 42 degrees from the direction directly opposite the Sun, with violet at a slightly smaller angle. The droplets send light in many directions; the rainbow is the bright concentration, not the only light they return.

## Why the light gathers near 42 degrees

Follow parallel rays of one colour into a round droplet, shifting their entry points from the middle towards the edge. After one internal reflection, their exit directions swing farther from the exact backward direction, reach a maximum angle, then swing back. For red light, that turning point is near 42 degrees.

Near the turning point, changing where a ray enters barely changes its exit angle. Many incoming rays therefore emerge within a narrow range of directions, concentrating light into a bright bow. Other rays return at smaller angles, but this one-reflection path has an outer angular limit. That helps explain the primary bow's sharp outer edge and softer inner edge. It does not mean the sky beyond the bow receives no other light.

## An angle can draw a circle

Picture a line from the Sun through your eye and onwards. Its far end points towards the **antisolar point**, directly opposite the Sun. Now keep your gaze about 42 degrees away from that line while sweeping it all the way round. The possible directions trace a circle.

In three dimensions those sightlines form the surface of a cone, with your eye at its tip. Droplets at different distances can contribute. They need not sit in a single flat ring, any more than the stars in a circular patch of sky need to be the same distance away.

The same reasoning explains the colour order. Red's slightly larger angle places it on the outside of the primary bow; violet's smaller angle puts it inside.

## The landscape cuts it short

With the Sun above the horizon, the antisolar point is below it. From ground level, much of the circle is hidden by the ground or lacks visible sunlit droplets. You see an arc. From an aircraft or another high viewpoint, suitable droplets below you can reveal the complete circle.

Moving your eye changes the cone's tip and therefore changes which droplets send the right light to you. People standing apart do not receive exactly the same rainbow rays, even when both bows appear over the same field.

The missing end is not a place you have failed to reach. It is where your present line of sight meets the landscape.
