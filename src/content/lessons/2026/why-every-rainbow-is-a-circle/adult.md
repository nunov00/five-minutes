A rainbow seems to be an arch fixed over the landscape. Move sideways, though, and you do not reach it. The rainbow moves with you because its geometry is centred on your eyes.

Sunlight enters millions of water droplets. At each droplet, the light bends as it enters the water, reflects from the back of the droplet, and bends again as it leaves. Different wavelengths bend by different amounts, so the returning light is separated into colours.

## One angle, many droplets

For a primary rainbow, red light reaches an observer from droplets roughly 42 degrees away from the direction directly opposite the Sun. Violet arrives at a slightly smaller angle. That is why red appears on the outside of the main bow and violet on the inside.

Now imagine every droplet that can send red light to one eye at that same angle. The possible rays form the surface of a cone with the observer at its tip and the cone's axis pointing directly away from the Sun. Looking through that cone, its rim appears as a circle.

The rainbow therefore has a centre: the **antisolar point**, the point exactly opposite the Sun from the observer. It is often below the horizon when the Sun is above it.

## Why we usually see only an arch

From the ground, the landscape and horizon hide the lower part of the circle. Droplets below that line are absent, blocked or simply out of view. From an aircraft or a high viewpoint, with illuminated droplets below the observer, the missing section can become visible and the rainbow may appear as a complete ring.

There is no single circular sheet hanging in the rain. Each observer receives light from a different set of droplets. Two people standing apart therefore see different rainbows, even while describing the same storm.

The arch is not half of the rainbow's real shape. It is the part of an observer-centred circle that the surroundings allow us to see.
