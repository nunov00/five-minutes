A rainbow seems to end behind a nearby tree. Could you run round the tree and stand inside the colours?

There is no coloured object waiting there. The tree stays put, but the droplets making your rainbow change as you move.

Sunlight bends when it enters a water droplet, reflects inside, then bends again when it leaves. Different colours bend by different amounts, so they leave along slightly different paths.

For the main rainbow, red light reaches you most strongly from droplets about 42 degrees away from the direction directly opposite the Sun. Violet appears at a slightly smaller angle, inside the red.

## Your eye is the centre of the view

Imagine looking straight away from the Sun, with sunlight coming from behind you. Tilt your gaze to one side, then sweep it around while keeping the same angle from that central direction. You have traced a circle in your view.

Droplets in those directions can send the rainbow colours to your eyes. They can be near or far away: they are not arranged in a giant hoop.

Why do you usually see only an arch? When the Sun is above the horizon, the centre of that circle is below it. The ground cuts off much of the view. From a plane, sunlit droplets below you can sometimes complete the ring.

Move sideways and all those angles are measured from a new eye position. A different set of droplets now supplies the light. Your friend across the field gets a view built around their own eyes.

Running towards the tree brings you closer to the tree. It does not bring you closer to a pot of colours, because the rainbow's apparent endpoint was never attached to the ground.
