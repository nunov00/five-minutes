A rainbow looks as if it has landed somewhere ahead of you. Try to walk toward it, however, and it keeps its distance. That is because the bow is not sitting at one address in the rain. It is built around where you are looking from.

Sunlight enters a raindrop, bends, reflects inside it, and bends again on the way out. The colours spread apart because each wavelength follows a slightly different path.

## Your eyes set the circle

For the main rainbow, red light usually reaches your eyes from droplets about 42 degrees away from the point directly opposite the Sun. Other colours arrive at nearby angles, with violet on the inner edge.

Picture an invisible line running from the Sun, through your head, and into the sky ahead. Now picture every droplet that sits at the same angle around that line and can return red light to you. Together those possible droplets form a cone, a little like the surface of a party hat with your eye at its tip.

The round rim of that cone is why the rainbow is a circle. Its centre is opposite the Sun, which is also why the Sun needs to be behind you.

## The horizon crops the picture

On the ground, the bottom of the circle is usually below the horizon or blocked by land. You see only the upper arc. From a plane, there can be sunlit droplets below as well as above you, so the complete ring may appear.

Move to a new place and the angles are measured from a new eye position. Different droplets now send the right light toward you. A friend several streets away may see a rainbow at the same time, but it is not made from exactly the same rays.

The rainbow does not have a hidden end or a reachable foot. The landscape has simply cropped a full circle that follows its observer.
