Step out of a shower into a breeze and your skin can feel suddenly cold. The air has not necessarily cooled. Water is changing state, and some of the energy for that change comes from you.

Turning liquid water into vapour requires energy to overcome attractions between its molecules. During evaporation, molecules escape from the surface and carry energy away. The energy can come from the remaining water, the skin beneath it and the surroundings. This is the cooling mechanism sweat uses.

## The air beside your skin matters

Evaporation does not require boiling. Water molecules have a range of energies, and some can escape while the bulk liquid remains far below its boiling point. Cooling can therefore happen continuously from a thin layer of sweat.

But the surface is not working alone. Water vapour in the adjacent air affects the net escape. At otherwise similar conditions, more humid surrounding air generally reduces evaporation. A breeze can carry the moist boundary layer away and replace it with less humid air, helping evaporation continue.

That explains the difference between sweating and being cooled by sweat. A drop that runs off has not provided the evaporative cooling it could have delivered by becoming vapour. Producing more liquid is not enough if the surroundings limit its evaporation.

The same physics helps explain why a wet cloth dries faster in moving, dry air and why evaporative coolers suit dry climates better than humid ones. Temperature alone does not describe the exchange: water, energy and the surrounding air all take part.
