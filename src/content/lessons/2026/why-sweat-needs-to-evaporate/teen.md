After a shower, a breeze can make you shiver even in a warm room. Why should moving warm air feel cold against wet skin?

The breeze helps water evaporate. Water needs energy to change from liquid into gas. Some of that energy comes from your skin, leaving it cooler. Sweat uses the same trick.

## The disappearing drop does the work

Water does not have to reach boiling point to evaporate. Its molecules have different energies. Some can escape from the surface while the rest remain liquid, carrying energy with them.

Imagine a drop of sweat taking two possible routes. It might evaporate from your arm, taking energy away. Or it might roll off onto the ground. The second route has not delivered the cooling that evaporation could have provided.

The surrounding air changes which route is easier. Air close to wet skin becomes moist. A breeze can move that layer away. Drier replacement air generally lets more water evaporate; already humid air makes net evaporation slower under otherwise similar conditions.

This is also why a wet sports shirt can take longer to dry in still, humid air than in a dry breeze. There is water ready to leave, but the air around it matters.

Sweat is the supply of water. Evaporation is the energy transfer. Looking wet tells you about the supply, not how effectively the transfer is happening.
