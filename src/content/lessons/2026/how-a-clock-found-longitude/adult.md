Latitude can be estimated from the height of the Sun or stars. Longitude—the east-west position—posed a harder problem at sea because it requires comparing two places at the same moment.

Earth rotates 360 degrees in 24 hours, or 15 degrees each hour. A navigator can find local noon when the Sun reaches its highest point. If a clock still shows noon at the reference port, the ship is on the same longitude. If it shows 2 p.m., the two-hour difference corresponds to 30 degrees.

The mathematics was straightforward; preserving the reference time was not. Pendulum clocks failed on rolling ships, and temperature and friction changed mechanical rates.

John Harrison developed marine timekeepers that resisted those disturbances. By 1761 his chronometer kept time on a voyage accurately enough to demonstrate a practical solution to the longitude problem.
