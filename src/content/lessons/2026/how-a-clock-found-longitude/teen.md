question: Why was an ordinary accurate clock on land not enough to solve longitude at sea?
answer: The clock had to preserve reference time despite a ship's motion, changing temperature and a voyage lasting weeks or months.
searchTerms: [longitude problem, ship navigation clock, marine chronometer]

A clock cannot see the ocean, so how could it tell a sailor where the ship was?

Earth makes one 360-degree rotation in 24 hours: 15 degrees each hour. A sailor could identify local noon when the Sun reached its highest point. The missing information was the time at a known starting longitude at that exact moment.

If the reference clock read 3 p.m. at local noon, the three-hour difference meant 45 degrees of longitude separated the ship from the reference.

But a sea clock had to remain accurate while being shaken, tilted and exposed to changing temperatures for weeks. Pendulum clocks were poor travellers. John Harrison spent decades developing marine timekeepers, and his 1761 chronometer demonstrated that reference time could survive the journey accurately enough for navigation.

**Takeaway:** longitude is partly a time problem, solved by carrying a reliable piece of home across the ocean.
