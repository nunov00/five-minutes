A fair coin has landed heads five times. Tails feels overdue. Yet if the tosses are independent, the next toss still has a one-half chance of tails.

**Independent** means that knowing earlier outcomes does not change the probability of the next one. The gambler's fallacy is the belief that an independent process must soon correct a streak.

## Balance without repayment

Suppose the next 100 tosses happen to produce exactly 50 heads and 50 tails. Combined with the first five heads, that gives 55 heads out of 105 tosses: about 52.4%.

The percentage has moved towards half. The five-head gap has not disappeared. No extra tails were needed. This is an illustrative result, not a prediction of exactly what those 100 tosses will produce.

The law of large numbers concerns the behaviour of averages across many trials. It does not assign the next toss the job of repairing the scoreboard.

## Check whether the process changes

Drawing coloured counters from a bag without replacing them is different. If you remove a red counter, the bag's contents change, so the next draw's probabilities may change too. The process retains a physical consequence of its past.

There is another distinction. If you do not know whether a coin is fair, a long run can give you evidence about that uncertainty. Learning about a possibly biased coin is not the same as expecting a known fair coin to repay a streak.

Before using a run of results to predict the next one, identify what connects them. A feeling that the sequence looks unbalanced is not itself a mechanism.
