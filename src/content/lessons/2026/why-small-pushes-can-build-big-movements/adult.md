A swing can rise higher after a series of modest pushes than after one large shove. The pushes arrive at useful points in its motion, adding energy repeatedly. Their timing lets separate efforts accumulate.

An oscillating system has a **natural frequency**: a rate at which it tends to move back and forth when disturbed. Repeated forcing near that frequency can produce a large response. That concentration of response is resonance.

## Energy in, energy out

The external force supplies the energy. Resonance does not manufacture it. For a swing, well-timed pushes add energy while friction and air resistance remove it. Push against the motion and you can remove energy instead of adding it.

The losses are called **damping**. In a simple model driven with a constant force amplitude, lower damping produces a taller, narrower response peak near the natural frequency. Greater damping reduces and broadens that peak. The plotted spring-and-mass example shows this without pretending that every real structure follows one simple curve.

For a damped system, motion does not increase without limit under a fixed repeating force. It can settle to a response where the energy supplied balances the energy lost. Real systems can also change behaviour as their movements become large.

That gives engineers several ways to address unwanted vibration: change the driving frequency, change the structure's natural response, or increase damping. First they must establish the cause; a loose part can rattle without resonance.

The useful question is not just how hard something is being pushed. It is how the timing of that push interacts with the system's motion and losses.
