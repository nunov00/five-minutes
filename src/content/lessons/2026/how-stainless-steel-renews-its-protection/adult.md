A scratched stainless-steel sink can keep resisting corrosion even though the scratch remains visible. Something has repaired itself, but it is not the shape of the sink.

Chromium in the alloy helps form an extremely thin, chromium-rich surface film. This **passive layer** slows corrosion by separating the underlying metal from its surroundings. It is a chemical barrier only a few nanometres thick, rather than a coat of paint.

## Repairing a defence

If a scratch exposes fresh metal, a protective film can form over it again when enough oxygen is available and the conditions are suitable. Oxygen in air or dissolved in water can support this renewal, known as repassivation.

The distinction matters. The surface becomes protected again, but any metal scraped away stays missing. Calling stainless steel “self-healing” describes renewal of the film, not recovery of a gouge or restoration of structural strength.

Nor is the protection unconditional. Chloride-rich environments can break down the film locally and allow small pits to develop. A tight crevice can create aggressive conditions and limit the oxygen available to maintain protection. Different stainless alloys and surface conditions behave differently under the same exposure.

A sink, a coastal fitting and a joint that traps salty water therefore present different jobs. The word “stainless” does not make their surroundings irrelevant.

When a material is said to repair itself, ask exactly what comes back: a barrier, a sealed crack, or the missing material itself. Here the remarkable part is a defence that can renew while the evidence of damage remains.
