A scratched phone screen stays scratched. So how can stainless steel be called “self-healing” while a mark on a sink remains perfectly visible?

The repair happens on a much smaller scale. Chromium mixed into the steel helps create a thin protective film at its surface. You cannot normally see this **passive layer**, but it slows the reactions that corrode the metal beneath it.

## Two different kinds of damage

Imagine a scratch scraping through the film and removing some steel. In suitable conditions, oxygen from the air or water helps a new protective film form along the exposed surface.

That restores the barrier. It does not put the scraped-out steel back. The groove remains, even when its surface is protected again. A film can renew without the whole object recovering its original shape.

There are limits. Salt contains chlorides that can damage the film in a small spot, allowing a pit to develop. A tight gap holding salty water can be especially troublesome because the local conditions can make protection harder to maintain. Different stainless alloys resist different conditions.

That is why “stainless” is not a promise that any part can be left in any environment forever. A kitchen sink and a seaside fitting do not face the same job.

The interesting question behind “self-healing” is what actually heals. For stainless steel, the answer is a microscopic defence. The scratch you can see and the protection you cannot see are different things.
