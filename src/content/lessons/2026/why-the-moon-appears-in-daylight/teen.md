You spot the Moon on the way home in the afternoon. Has it turned up early? Only if you expect the Sun and Moon to work opposite shifts.

The Moon reflects sunlight. If it is above your horizon and bright enough against the sky, you can see it while the Sun is still up. Daytime does not automatically hide it.

## Why its shape changes

As the Moon moves around Earth, we see different amounts of its sunlit half. That changing shape is its **phase**. An ordinary crescent is not Earth's shadow covering most of the Moon; our viewing angle has changed.

Around first quarter, an afternoon is often a good time to spot it. Around last quarter, try a morning. A full Moon is roughly opposite the Sun and is mostly a night-time sight. Near new Moon, it is near the Sun in our sky and its bright side faces mostly away from us.

Those are clues rather than exact instructions: the date and your location affect rise and set times. Check a local sky calendar, and look well away from the Sun. Never point binoculars or a telescope near it.

A daytime Moon is a small reminder to question a familiar picture. The Moon does not make its own light or wait for a night shift. It follows the same geometry all day.
