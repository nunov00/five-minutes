The Moon in a blue afternoon sky is not in the wrong place. The familiar Sun-for-day, Moon-for-night picture is simply too restrictive.

Two questions matter: is the Moon above your local horizon, and can you see its illuminated surface against the sky? The Sun and Moon can both be above that horizon at once. The Moon reflects sunlight and can be bright enough to remain visible in daylight.

## Position and phase are different questions

Except during a lunar eclipse, sunlight illuminates roughly half the Moon. Its **phase** is the changing portion of that lit half visible from Earth as the Moon orbits. Ordinary phases are not Earth's shadow moving across it.

Around first quarter, the Moon rises roughly at midday and is often visible during the afternoon and evening. Around last quarter, it rises roughly at midnight and remains up into the morning. These are broad patterns, not a local timetable.

A full Moon is approximately opposite the Sun in our sky, making it mainly a night-time sight. Near new Moon, it appears near the Sun and its lit side faces mostly away from us, making it difficult to see.

Use a local sky calendar for exact times. Look well away from the Sun; never search near it with binoculars or a telescope. The useful insight is not an unusual exception to night-time astronomy. It is that horizon, illumination and viewing angle answer different questions.
