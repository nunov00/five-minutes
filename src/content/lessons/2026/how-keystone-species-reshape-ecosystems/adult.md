---
title: How One Species Can Reshape an Ecosystem
description: A keystone species has an ecological influence far larger than its abundance suggests, often through a chain of indirect effects.
takeaway: Food webs respond through relationships, so changing one influential species can transform many others.
question: How can a predator indirectly increase the amount of plant life in an ecosystem?
answer: By limiting herbivores, the predator reduces grazing pressure, allowing more vegetation to survive and support other species.
searchTerms: [keystone species, trophic cascade, sea otters kelp]
---
Sea otters do not eat kelp. Yet removing otters can help destroy a kelp forest.

Otters prey on sea urchins, and urchins graze on kelp. With fewer otters, urchin populations and feeding pressure can rise. Kelp declines, removing food and habitat used by many other organisms. A change at one level has cascaded through the food web.

Ecologists call this pattern a **trophic cascade**. A species whose influence is unusually large relative to its abundance may be described as a **keystone species**, like the central stone that helps an arch hold its form.

The label is useful, but ecosystems are not simple rows of dominoes. Effects depend on place, competitors, climate, disease and human activity. Calling one species “the key” can hide those interactions.

The deeper lesson is about networks: direct relationships create indirect consequences. Protecting or removing one population may change organisms it never touches.

**Takeaway:** in a food web, ecological importance is not measured only by how many individuals exist.
