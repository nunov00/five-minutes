Moving heat from a warm room into a colder outdoors feels natural: leave a door open in winter and it happens by itself. A heat pump makes heat travel the other way. It does so by spending electrical energy, much as a water pump spends energy to move water uphill.

The working fluid is a **refrigerant**. By changing its pressure, the machine changes the temperatures at which it can absorb and release heat.

## A cycle with two different sides

On the outdoor side, the refrigerant is kept cold enough to absorb heat from the surrounding air, ground or water. Even cold outdoor air still contains thermal energy. The warmed refrigerant travels to a compressor, which raises its pressure and temperature.

Now hotter than the indoor heating system, the refrigerant passes through a second heat exchanger and releases heat into the building. It then moves through an expansion device. Its pressure and temperature fall, preparing it to collect heat outside again.

The refrigerant circulates; it is not used up on each trip. The cycle keeps creating a cold side that can collect heat and a hot side that can release it.

## Why the output can be larger than the input

An electric resistance heater turns its electrical input directly into heat. A heat pump uses electricity mainly to operate the compressor and move heat that already exists elsewhere.

There is no free energy. The heat delivered indoors has two contributors: heat collected outside and the electrical work supplied to the machine. That is why a heat pump can deliver several units of heat for each unit of electricity it uses without breaking conservation of energy.

Many systems can reverse the cycle in summer. Then the indoor heat exchanger collects heat and the outdoor one releases it—the same basic process used by an air conditioner or refrigerator.
