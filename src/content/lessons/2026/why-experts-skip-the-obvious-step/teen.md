You ask for help adding a third and a sixth. “Just find a common denominator,” comes the answer. That may be correct, but if you do not know what the phrase means, you are still stuck.

Try a different route. Thirds and sixths are different-sized pieces. Split each third into two sixths, and now both fractions describe pieces of the same size. One third becomes two sixths; adding another sixth gives three sixths. Only then introduce the name for the method.

The second explanation supplies a bridge instead of simply naming the destination.

## Why someone can forget the bridge

Knowing something can make it hard to judge what it looks like without that knowledge. This is called the **curse of knowledge**. Research has demonstrated this difficulty in tasks where people have different information; the fractions example is an illustration, not that experiment.

It does not prove that every confusing explanation comes from this bias. But it gives you a useful question: what does the speaker already know that the listener still needs?

When you teach someone a game, try explaining one turn before listing every special rule. Then let them try a turn and describe their choices. When you are the learner, “Why does that step work?” is a reasonable question. A missing explanation is not evidence that you are incapable of understanding it.
