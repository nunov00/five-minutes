“The service is down because the DNS cache is stale.” To one colleague, that explains the outage. To another, it simply replaces one mystery with three unfamiliar terms.

The missing bridge might be this: computers use names to look up addresses; a stored address is out of date; requests are going to the wrong place. The technical label becomes useful after the mechanism has somewhere to attach.

This illustrates the **curse of knowledge**: once you possess information, setting it aside to judge another person's position can be difficult. Experiments in economic settings found that better-informed people could not fully disregard their extra information even when doing so was in their interest. Explaining a system presents a related challenge: your own starting point is not necessarily your listener's.

## Find the missing bridge

Imagine writing instructions to restore a backup. “Choose the recovery point” may be enough for an experienced colleague. A newcomer may first need to know that several saved states exist, and that the choice determines which later changes will be absent after recovery.

Adding more detail about the button does not explain that missing decision. Nor does repeating the instruction more slowly.

A useful check is to give the listener a small example and ask what they would do next, and why. Their reasoning can reveal the gap more clearly than “Does that make sense?” This is a practical check, not a guarantee that every misunderstanding will surface.

The aim is not to ban specialist terms or explain everything from the beginning. Establish which ideas are shared, supply the missing step, then let the precise vocabulary do its job.
