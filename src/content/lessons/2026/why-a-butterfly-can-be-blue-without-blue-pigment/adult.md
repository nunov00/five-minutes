In a demonstration documented by the NISE Network, a blue morpho butterfly wing turns green where isopropyl alcohol wets it. As the alcohol evaporates, the blue returns. The wing has not grown a new colour in those few moments. What changed was what filled its tiny gaps.

A blue pigment would make an object look blue by absorbing other parts of the light falling on it. A blue morpho wing works differently. Its bright blue comes mainly from the arrangement of material on its surface. This is **structural colour**: colour made by the way light interacts with a structure, rather than by a blue colouring substance.

White light contains a mixture of wavelengths. A wavelength is the distance from one wave crest to the next; blue light has a shorter wavelength than red light. The wing does not need to turn red light into blue. It can send more of the incoming blue light towards your eyes.

## How tiny shelves select a colour

The wing is covered with overlapping scales. On the scales of blue morphos such as *Morpho didius*, narrow ridges carry stacks of thin shelves. These shelves are made of cuticle, the material forming the insect's outer covering. Air fills the spaces between them. Their spacing is comparable to the wavelengths of visible light, so this tiny architecture affects how the light waves combine.

Some light is reflected at the boundaries between cuticle and air. Reflections from different depths have travelled different distances. For some wavelengths, their crests arrive together and their troughs arrive together: the waves reinforce each other. For others, crests and troughs partly oppose each other, reducing the reflection. This addition of waves is called **interference**. The layer spacing and the optical properties of the materials help determine which wavelengths are reinforced. In a dry blue morpho scale, blue is strongly favoured.

Real scales are more complicated than a neat pile of identical sheets. The shelves have limited widths, and neighbouring ridges differ in height. Light also spreads as it meets these small structures, an effect called **diffraction**. Together, these features affect the directions in which the blue can be seen. ## Change the gaps, change the colour

Light travels differently through air and alcohol. When alcohol enters the spaces between the shelves, it changes the optical properties of those spaces. Even with the solid structure still present, the conditions for reinforcing one wavelength have changed. The NISE demonstration reports a green appearance in the wet region. The liquid need not be green: it changes how the wing reflects the light already arriving.

When the alcohol evaporates, air returns to the gaps. The original optical conditions return too, and the wing looks blue again. This is a reported observation, not an experiment you need to repeat. The blue–green–blue sequence connects an invisible change inside the structure to a visible change in colour.

That reversible change is evidence to interpret alongside microscopy and optical measurements. By itself, a colour returning after drying would not prove that pigments played no part: chemicals can also change colour reversibly. Here, the observed shelves and measured reflections provide the structural explanation.

Nor does “without blue pigment” mean “without pigment”. Brown pigment is present in wings of species such as *M. didius*. It absorbs background light and helps the blue reflection stand out. Under transmitted light—light passing through the wing—the brown can become visible. The brilliant surface and the brown pigment are different contributions to the same wing's appearance.

The surprise is therefore in the gaps as well as the solid material. The same tiny shelves can favour a different reflected colour when their surroundings change. The blue returns when air does, without the butterfly needing a fresh supply of blue dye.
