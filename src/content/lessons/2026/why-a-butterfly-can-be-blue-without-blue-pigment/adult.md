A blue morpho wing turns green where clear alcohol wets it, then blue again as it dries. A museum activity guide reports the change. How can a colour disappear and return without the wing growing anything new?

A blue pigment makes an object look blue by absorbing other parts of the light falling on it. A blue morpho wing gets its bright blue mainly from the arrangement of material on its surface. This is **structural colour**: colour made by the way light interacts with a structure.

White light contains a mixture of wavelengths. A wavelength is the distance from one wave crest to the next. Blue light has shorter wavelengths than green, which has shorter wavelengths than red. The wing can send more incoming blue light towards your eyes without turning another colour of light into blue.

## How tiny shelves select a colour

The wing carries overlapping scales. In blue morphos such as *Morpho didius*, narrow ridges on those scales carry thin shelves of cuticle, the material forming the insect’s outer covering. Air fills the gaps. The shelf-and-gap spacing is comparable to the wavelengths of visible light, far too small to see with the naked eye. Look at the spaces as well as the shelves in the drawing.

Some light reflects at the boundaries between cuticle and air. Reflections from different depths travel different distances. For some wavelengths, the crests arrive together and the troughs arrive together, strengthening the reflection. For others, crests and troughs partly oppose each other. This addition of waves is called **interference**. The shelf spacing and travel through each material help determine which wavelengths line up. In a dry blue morpho scale, blue is strongly favoured.

Real scales are not perfect stacks of identical sheets. Their shelves have limited widths, and neighbouring ridges differ in height. Light also spreads as it meets these small structures, an effect called **diffraction**. These features help determine the directions in which the blue can be seen; the drawing simplifies their combined effect.

## Change the gaps, change the colour

Alcohol slows light more than air does. Light crossing an alcohol-filled gap therefore takes longer to travel the same distance. Reflections following different paths gain different delays, changing which wavelengths arrive together and strengthen each other. The strongest reflection shifts towards longer wavelengths—towards green in the reported demonstration. The wave comparison above shows this change: blue reflections are more in step in the dry case, green in the wet case. The shelves have not moved. The clear liquid changes how reflected waves combine, rather than acting as a green dye that absorbs unwanted colours.

When the alcohol evaporates, air returns. The shorter crossing time restores the pattern that favours blue. Microscopy and optical measurements support this explanation; the colour’s return alone would not rule out a chemical change. The diagram connects the invisible change in the gaps to the visible blue–green–blue sequence.

Structural colour does not mean a pigment-free wing. In *M. didius*, brown pigment absorbs light that would otherwise weaken the contrast of the blue reflection. When light passes through the wing rather than reflecting from it, the brown can become visible.

## From colour to a possible sensor

That sensitivity to the surroundings suggests another job for a wing structure: helping distinguish liquids. In their 2022 paper, Dong and colleagues used a model to look for lighting and viewing angles that would make different liquids easier to distinguish by their reflected colours, while tolerating small angle errors. The paper left those proposed measurement improvements for future experiments to test. The research question reverses the opening puzzle: if a liquid can change the colour, could the colour help tell us about the liquid?
