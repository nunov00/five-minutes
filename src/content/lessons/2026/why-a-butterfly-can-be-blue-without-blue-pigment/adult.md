A museum activity guide describes a blue morpho butterfly wing turning green where isopropyl alcohol wets it. As the alcohol evaporates, the blue returns. The wing has not grown a new colour in those few moments. What changed was what filled its tiny gaps.

A blue pigment would make an object look blue by absorbing other parts of the light falling on it. A blue morpho wing works differently. Its bright blue comes mainly from the arrangement of material on its surface. This is **structural colour**: colour made by the way light interacts with a structure, rather than by a blue colouring substance.

White light contains a mixture of wavelengths. A wavelength is the distance from one wave crest to the next; blue light has a shorter wavelength than red light. Green light has a longer wavelength than blue and a shorter one than red. The wing does not need to turn red light into blue. It can send more of the incoming blue light towards your eyes.

## How tiny shelves select a colour

The wing is covered with overlapping scales. On the scales of blue morphos such as *Morpho didius*, narrow ridges carry stacks of thin shelves. These shelves are made of cuticle, the material forming the insect's outer covering. Air fills the spaces between them. Their spacing is comparable to the wavelengths of visible light, so this tiny architecture affects how the light waves combine.

Some light is reflected at the boundaries between cuticle and air. Reflections from different depths have travelled different distances. For some wavelengths, their crests arrive together and their troughs arrive together: the waves reinforce each other. For others, crests and troughs partly oppose each other, reducing the reflection. This addition of waves is called **interference**. The layer spacing and how light travels through each material help determine which wavelengths are reinforced. In a dry blue morpho scale, blue is strongly favoured.

Real scales are more complicated than a neat pile of identical sheets. The shelves have limited widths, and neighbouring ridges differ in height. Light also spreads as it meets these small structures, an effect called **diffraction**. Together, these features affect the directions in which the blue can be seen. Peacock feathers also show structural colour, although their tiny colour-making structures are different.

## Change the gaps, change the colour

Alcohol slows light more than air does. In an alcohol-filled gap, light therefore takes longer to cross the same distance. The gap acts like a thicker layer for the wave, even though the shelves have not moved. This extra delay changes which reflected waves arrive together. The reinforced colour shifts towards longer wavelengths—towards green in this demonstration. The alcohol is clear; it changes which part of the incoming light the wing reflects most strongly.

When the alcohol evaporates, air returns to the gaps. Light crosses them more quickly again, restoring the pattern of reflections that favours blue. This is a reported observation, not an experiment you need to repeat. The blue–green–blue sequence connects an invisible change inside the structure to a visible change in colour.

That reversible change is evidence to interpret alongside microscopy and optical measurements. By itself, a colour returning after drying would not prove that pigments played no part: chemicals can also change colour reversibly. Here, the observed shelves and measured reflections provide the structural explanation.

Nor does “without blue pigment” mean “without pigment”. Brown pigment is present in wings of species such as *M. didius*. It absorbs green and red light that would otherwise make the blue reflection less distinct. Under transmitted light—light passing through the wing—the brown can become visible. The brilliant surface and the brown pigment are different contributions to the same wing's appearance.

The surprise is therefore in the gaps as well as the solid material. The same tiny shelves can favour a different reflected colour when their surroundings change. The blue returns when air does, without the butterfly needing a fresh supply of blue dye.
