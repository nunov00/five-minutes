A metal chair leg feels colder than its wooden seat, even after both have spent the night in the same room. A thermometer can report the same temperature for each. Your hand is responding to a different question: how quickly is it losing heat?

Your skin is usually warmer than a room-temperature object. On contact, energy moves from the warmer skin into the cooler material. Metal carries that energy away from the contact region readily, allowing heat to keep leaving your skin. Wood conducts it much less readily, so the surface next to your hand warms more locally and the transfer slows.

## A sensation of transfer

**Thermal conductivity** describes how readily a material carries heat through itself. It helps explain why a metal pan spreads heat while a wooden handle slows its passage. The touch sensation also depends on the object's thickness, its ability to store energy and the quality of contact. A thin sheet and a massive block need not feel identical, even when made from the same metal.

Reverse the temperature difference and the direction of heat flow reverses. Metal hotter than your skin can transfer heat into it quickly. There is no special coldness stored inside metal; the same ability to transfer energy explains both sensations.

This is why touch is an unreliable thermometer. A material can change the reading your senses give without changing the temperature a thermometer would measure. The chair's seat and leg disagree with your hand, not with each other.
