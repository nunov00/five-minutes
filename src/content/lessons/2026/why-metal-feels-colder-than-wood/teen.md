You touch a metal chair leg, then the wooden seat. The metal feels colder. Has it somehow found a colder part of the room?

Not necessarily. Leave both in the same steady room long enough and they can reach the same temperature. The difference appears when your warmer hand touches them: heat leaves your skin faster through the metal.

## Follow the energy

Heat moves from warmer things towards cooler things. Metal carries that energy away from the spot under your hand easily. Wood slows its journey, so the small patch next to your hand warms without spreading the energy as quickly through the rest of the seat.

That difference is linked to **thermal conductivity**: how readily a material carries heat. Your skin cools faster against the metal, and you interpret that as colder.

Now imagine the materials are hotter than your hand. Energy would move the other way, into your skin. The metal could feel hotter for the same reason it felt colder before. This is a thought experiment, not a reason to touch hot objects.

The material is only part of the story. Thickness and contact matter too: a tiny piece cannot take in as much energy as a large block before warming up.

A thermometer measures temperature. Your hand experiences an exchange of energy. Those are related, but they are different measurements. That is how two parts of one chair can tell your senses different stories.
