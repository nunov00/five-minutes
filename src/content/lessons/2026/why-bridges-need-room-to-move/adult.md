A deliberate gap in a bridge can be part of what keeps it sound. Steel changes length as its temperature changes. Preventing all movement does not remove that tendency; it can turn it into large internal forces.

For most solids under ordinary conditions, heating increases the average separation of neighbouring atoms. The change is small over a spoon and appreciable over a long span. Engineers must decide where that movement can go.

## A small fraction over a large distance

Take an illustrative 100-metre steel length that is free to expand. Using a representative coefficient of 0.000012 per degree Celsius, a 30°C rise gives:

100 m × 0.000012 per °C × 30°C = 0.036 m, or 3.6 cm.

The same fractional change across twice the starting length gives twice the movement. This is a scale estimate, not a specification for a real bridge joint: material properties, temperature differences across the structure and the arrangement of restraints all matter.

An expansion joint allows specified relative movement while maintaining a usable road surface. Other parts of a bridge also help manage motion and loads. The important design question is where movement is allowed and where forces are carried, not whether a structure can be made perfectly still.

The allowance must keep working after construction. A joint clogged with debris or damaged by corrosion may no longer move or drain as intended. Inspection therefore considers its condition and measures its opening alongside temperature.

The gap is doing a job. A structure can be stronger in service because its design permits a little movement.
