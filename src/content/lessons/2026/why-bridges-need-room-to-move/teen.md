A bridge can look perfectly still while quietly changing shape. Sunshine warms the deck, a cold night cools it, and heavy vehicles make it bend and rotate a little.

Warm steel gets longer. A useful rough estimate is about one millimetre of growth for every metre of steel when its temperature rises by 10°C. Multiply that across a long bridge and a seasonal temperature swing, and the ends need real room to travel.

Trying to clamp the whole bridge in place would not cancel that change. It would convert the blocked movement into stress, rather like twisting a ruler while holding both ends.

## The parts that give

The line across the road near the end of a bridge may be an **expansion joint**. The joint opens and closes as the deck changes length, while its surface lets wheels cross. A seal can also divert water and grit away from vulnerable parts below.

The deck rests on **bearings**. They carry enormous weight, but some can slide slightly or let a beam rotate. Engineers choose which directions each bearing may move so the whole bridge follows a planned path.

Heat is only part of the story. Concrete slowly shrinks and changes shape under long-lasting loads, traffic flexes the deck, and the ground can settle. A joint must cope with the combined range, not just one sunny afternoon.

Some bridges avoid visible deck joints because joints wear and can leak. Their decks still expand and contract; flexible bridge ends or abutments take up the movement instead.

So the gap is not evidence that builders failed to connect the bridge. It is evidence that they expected the bridge to behave like real material rather than an unchanging model.
