A bump as a car crosses a bridge may come from an expansion joint: a place designed to let parts move. A gap can look like a weakness, but here allowing movement helps protect the structure.

Steel usually becomes a little longer when it warms and shorter when it cools. Neighbouring atoms end up slightly farther apart on average as the steel heats. Add those tiny changes along a whole bridge and the result becomes noticeable.

## How much is “a little”?

For an illustrative 100-metre steel length, a 30°C temperature rise can mean about 3.6 centimetres of extra length if it is free to expand. That estimate uses a typical steel expansion coefficient; it is not a rule for choosing any particular bridge gap.

Across 200 metres of the same freely expanding steel, the same temperature rise would give about 7.2 centimetres. The material has not changed its behaviour. There is simply more length over which the change adds up.

What if you clamp the ends so the steel cannot get longer? That does not switch expansion off. The restraint creates forces inside the material, rather than allowing the full change in length.

Engineers design joints and supports so the whole bridge handles movement and loads safely. Those parts need maintenance too: trapped debris can interfere with a joint, and damaged seals can let water reach parts below.

The bridge is not meant to behave like one immovable lump. It is meant to handle the conditions it will actually meet.
