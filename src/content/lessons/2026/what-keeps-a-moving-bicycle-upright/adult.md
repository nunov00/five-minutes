A bicycle is easy to tip when it stands still. Yet a bicycle rolling fast enough can sometimes recover from a sideways shove with nobody on the saddle. In 2011, researchers built one that did this even after they cancelled an effect often credited with keeping bicycles upright.[^1]

## Move the wheels towards the fall

Imagine balancing a broom upright on your palm. If the top starts falling left, you move your hand left to catch up with it. A bicycle can do something similar: if it starts leaning left, steering left curves the path of the wheels towards the falling frame. That can bring the support back underneath it.[^1][^2]

A rider can make the correction. A riderless bicycle needs its own lean to produce a change in steering. It also needs the right amount of correction at the right time. Turning towards a lean alone does not guarantee that it will recover.[^1]

## The bicycle built to test the explanation

A spinning wheel has a **gyroscopic effect**: changing the direction of its axle can produce a turning effect elsewhere in the bicycle. Another influence comes from **trail**. On many bicycles, the front tyre touches the ground behind the point where the steering axis would meet it. That offset affects how a force at the tyre turns the front assembly.[^1][^2]

The researchers built a bicycle that looked more like a small scooter. Extra wheels spun in the opposite direction to cancel the gyroscopic effect from the road wheels. The front tyre touched slightly *ahead* of where the steering axis met the ground, so the usual positive trail was absent too. When researchers gave the coasting machine a sideways hit above about 2.3 metres per second, it returned to upright, straight rolling. In a separate coast-down it began to fall below about 2 metres per second. Those speeds describe this machine and test, not a minimum for riding.[^1][^2]

## Why this one steered

The researchers put the weight of the steering assembly low down and ahead of the steering axis. Think of a short pencil and a long broom balanced upright: if both start to tip, the short one tends to fall faster. The low front assembly tends to fall faster than the higher rear frame. The parts are joined at the steering hinge, so that difference helps turn the front wheel towards the fall. Once the wheel turns, its path can move underneath the frame, like your palm moving to catch the broom.[^1][^2]

The experiment gives a way to test the explanation. If a bicycle can recover with those two familiar effects taken away, neither is essential. Both can still matter on an ordinary bicycle. What the machine needs is a steering response that catches the lean before it grows.[^1]
