On a cold morning, you may find a bedroom window covered in tiny drops even though the walls, bed and books are dry. The water did not leak through the glass. It came from **water vapour** that was already mixed invisibly through the indoor air.

The glass creates a special cold zone. Air touching it loses heat, while air farther into the room stays warmer. This thin cooled layer can behave differently from the room you feel.

## The dew-point line

At any temperature, there is a limit to how much water vapour can remain mixed through the air. **Relative humidity** compares the amount present with that temperature's limit. Cool the same air and the limit falls, so the percentage rises even though no new water has entered.

The **dew point** is the temperature where that air reaches 100% relative humidity. Cool it below that point and some vapour changes phase into liquid water. A cold drink “sweats” for the same reason: the drops come from the surrounding air, not through the cup.

Suppose the room air's dew point is 16°C. A pane with an inside surface at 18°C stays above the threshold. A pane at 12°C cools nearby air below it, so droplets can form. The rest of the room can remain warm enough to feel comfortable.

## Moisture and temperature work together

A shower, cooking pot or clothes rack can add vapour, raising the dew point. Colder weather can pull more heat through a window, lowering its inside-surface temperature. Either change can make the two temperatures cross.

This explains why “just wipe it” is temporary: wiping removes drops but not the vapour or cold surface that produced them. Ventilation can carry moist indoor air away, while better-insulating glass can keep the inside pane warmer. Neither is magic; condensation depends on the actual moisture and temperatures.

The common shortcut “the room is damp” misses the small-scale mechanism. The window is wet because the air touching it crossed a temperature threshold that the bulk of the room never reached.
