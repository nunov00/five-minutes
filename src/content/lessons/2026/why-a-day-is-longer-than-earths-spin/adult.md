Earth completes a turn relative to the distant stars in about 23 hours 56 minutes. Yet our clocks call a day 24 hours. The missing four minutes are not a correction for an inaccurate clock. They come from asking what Earth should line up with.

A **sidereal day** measures rotation against the stars. Imagine noting when a particular distant star crosses the north-south line in your sky. It will cross that line again about 23 hours 56 minutes later.

A **solar day** uses the Sun instead. It measures the interval from one solar noon to the next, when the Sun crosses that same line.

## The extra turn

During one rotation, Earth does not stay in the same place. It travels about one 365th of the way around the Sun, close to one degree of its orbit. After Earth has turned once relative to the stars, the Sun is therefore not yet back on the same north-south line.

Earth must rotate roughly one extra degree. The planet turns about 15 degrees per hour, so that extra degree takes close to four minutes. Add those minutes to the sidereal rotation and the result is the familiar 24-hour solar day.

Over a year, the small extra turns add up to about one complete rotation. That is why Earth makes roughly 366 rotations relative to the stars during a year containing about 365 solar days.

## Why clocks use an average

The interval between actual solar noons is not perfectly constant. Earth's orbit is not circular, and its axis is tilted, so the apparent Sun moves across the sky at a slightly changing rate through the year.

Civil time therefore follows the **mean solar day**: a uniform 24-hour average rather than every small variation shown by a sundial.

There is no single useful answer to “How long does Earth take to turn?” until the reference point is named. Against the stars it is about 23 hours 56 minutes; against the Sun, averaged across the year, it is 24 hours.
