Try this with a chair and a lamp. Let the chair be Earth, the lamp be the Sun and a mark on a distant wall be a star.

Spin the chair until you face the wall mark again. That is like one rotation measured against distant stars. On Earth it takes about 23 hours 56 minutes and is called a **sidereal day**.

But Earth is not spinning in one fixed spot. While it turns, it also moves along its orbit around the Sun. To copy that, move your chair a small step around the lamp while you spin.

Once you face the wall mark again, you will not quite face the lamp. You need a little extra turn.

## Where the four minutes come from

Each day, Earth travels close to one degree around its orbit. After one star-based rotation, it must therefore turn roughly one degree farther for the Sun to return to the same position in the sky.

Earth rotates about 15 degrees in an hour. One extra degree takes close to four minutes. That turns a 23-hour-56-minute sidereal day into a roughly 24-hour **solar day**.

Those tiny extra turns build up. Across one trip around the Sun, they add roughly one whole rotation. Earth therefore completes about 366 rotations relative to the stars while we count about 365 solar days.

Real Sun-based days also vary slightly through the year because Earth's orbit is not a perfect circle and its axis is tilted. Our clocks avoid speeding up and slowing down with a sundial. They use a uniform 24-hour average called the **mean solar day**.

So both numbers are right. The answer changes because “one turn” can mean lining up again with the stars or lining up again with the Sun.
