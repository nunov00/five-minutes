Earth completes a spin in about 23 hours 56 minutes. Yet a day on your clock lasts 24 hours. The planet has finished turning before your day has finished.

Imagine a small arrow painted on Earth, facing the Sun at noon. Keep track of that arrow against very distant stars. After one complete spin it points in its original direction again. Astronomers call this roughly 23-hour-56-minute interval a **sidereal day**.

But the Sun is a different target. While Earth spins, it also moves around the Sun. That journey changes the direction from Earth to the Sun, just as walking past a lamp changes the direction you need to face to look at it.

After the arrow has returned to its old star direction, it needs a little extra turn to face the Sun again. That second finish line gives us a **solar day**, measured from one solar noon to the next.

## Four minutes of catching up

Earth moves roughly one degree around its orbit each day. It spins about 15 degrees an hour, so catching up by one extra degree takes about four minutes.

The extra turns are small, but there is a whole orbit's worth of them in a year. Add them together and Earth makes roughly 366 spins against the stars while we count about 365 Sun-based days.

Actual solar days vary slightly because Earth's orbit is not perfectly circular and its axis is tilted. The **mean solar day** is the 24-hour average, rather than every day's exact sundial interval.

So the clock has not added four spare minutes. It is waiting for Earth to face a target that moved while the planet was turning.
